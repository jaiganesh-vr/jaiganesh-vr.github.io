BM25 and Hybrid Search

Algorithms Retrieval

Jun 09, 2026

Embedding search made retrieval feel new, but a decades-old keyword algorithm still outperforms it on a surprising share of queries. Most strong retrieval systems today use both.

How BM25 Scores Documents

BM25 ranks documents by how well their words match the query's words. For each query term, it combines three ideas:

  • Term frequency: a document that mentions the term more is more relevant, but with diminishing returns. The tenth mention adds much less than the second.
  • Inverse document frequency: rare terms matter more than common ones. Matching "kubernetes" says more than matching "system".
  • Length normalisation: long documents naturally contain more words, so their scores are adjusted down.

Two parameters control this: k₁, usually between 1.2 and 2, sets how quickly term frequency saturates; b, usually 0.75, sets how strongly length is normalised.

Where Keywords Win

Embeddings capture meaning, which is their strength and their weakness. They can blur exactly the details that matter for some queries:

  • Product codes, error messages and IDs like ERR_4031.
  • Names of people, libraries and functions.
  • Rare technical terms the embedding model saw little of.

BM25 matches these exactly. Embeddings match paraphrases and concepts, like a query about "cancelling a subscription" finding a document titled "ending your membership".

Keywords find what you said. Embeddings find what you meant.

Hybrid Search

Hybrid search runs both retrievers and merges their results. The simplest robust method is reciprocal rank fusion:

score(d) = Σ 1 / (k + rankᵢ(d))

Each retriever contributes based on the rank it gave the document, with k typically 60. Using ranks rather than raw scores avoids the problem that BM25 scores and cosine similarities are on incomparable scales.

Adding a Reranker

Hybrid retrieval gathers a strong candidate set. A cross-encoder reranker can then read each query-document pair together and reorder the top results more precisely. Retrieve broadly, then rank carefully.

Closing Thought

Retrieval quality usually limits a RAG system more than the language model does. Pairing an old, reliable algorithm with modern embeddings is one of the cheapest improvements available.
