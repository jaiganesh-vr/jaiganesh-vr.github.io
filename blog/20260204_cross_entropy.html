Cross-Entropy: The Loss Behind Every Language Model

Mathematics Training

Feb 04, 2026

Strip away the scale and engineering, and pre-training a language model is the minimisation of a single quantity: cross-entropy loss on the next token. It is worth understanding exactly what that number measures.

The Definition

At each position, the model outputs a probability distribution over the vocabulary. The training data tells us which token actually came next. Cross-entropy loss for that position is:

loss = −log p(correct token)

If the model gave the correct token probability 1, the loss is 0. If it gave it probability 0.5, the loss is about 0.69. If it gave it 0.01, the loss is about 4.6. The total loss is averaged over every position in the batch.

Why the Logarithm

The log punishes confident mistakes severely. Assigning the right answer a probability of 0.001 costs far more than assigning it 0.1, which pushes the model to keep some probability on plausible alternatives instead of betting everything on one guess.

The logarithm also turns products into sums. The probability of a whole sequence is the product of per-token probabilities, so its log is a sum of per-token terms, which is exactly what gets averaged.

Where the Name Comes From

In information theory, entropy measures the average surprise of a distribution. Cross-entropy measures the average surprise when outcomes come from the true distribution, but you predict them using another one.

Cross-entropy equals the true entropy plus the KL divergence between the two distributions. Since the data's entropy is fixed, minimising cross-entropy is the same as making the model's distribution as close as possible to the data's.

Reading the Number

  • Baseline: a model guessing uniformly over a 50,000-token vocabulary has a loss of ln(50,000), about 10.8.
  • Units: with natural logs, loss is measured in nats. Divide by ln 2 to get bits per token.
  • Perplexity: exponentiating the loss gives perplexity, an easier-to-read measure of how many choices the model is effectively hesitating between.

Loss values are only comparable between models that share a tokenizer. A tokenizer with larger tokens predicts fewer, harder tokens, so its per-token loss is not directly comparable.

Closing Thought

Everything a language model can do emerges from pushing this one number down across trillions of tokens. It is a remarkably simple objective for such a wide range of resulting behavior.
