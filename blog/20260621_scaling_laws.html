Scaling Laws, Briefly

Machine Learning Research

Jun 21, 2026

One of the most consequential findings in modern AI is that language model performance improves predictably with scale. Not just better with more, but along smooth curves that can be measured on small models and extrapolated to large ones.

The Original Finding

Early scaling studies trained many models of different sizes and found that loss falls as a power law in three quantities:

  • Number of parameters.
  • Amount of training data, in tokens.
  • Amount of compute used for training.

On a log-log plot, each relationship is close to a straight line, across many orders of magnitude.

Predictability turned model training from an experiment into an investment.

Why This Matters

If performance can be forecast, labs can decide how large a model to train before spending the budget. Small pilot runs predict the loss of runs hundreds of times larger, which reduces risk and justified the enormous training runs of recent years.

The Chinchilla Correction

The early laws suggested putting most extra compute into bigger models. A later study, known for its model Chinchilla, revisited this with more careful experiments and found that parameters and data should grow roughly together. Its rule of thumb was about 20 training tokens per parameter for compute-optimal training.

Many earlier models had been undertrained: too large for the data they saw. Chinchilla matched much larger models while being smaller and cheaper to run.

Beyond Compute-Optimal

Compute-optimal is optimal for training cost, not for total cost. A model that will serve billions of requests is cheaper overall if it is smaller and trained on far more tokens than the 20-to-1 ratio suggests. Many modern models are trained well past that ratio for this reason.

The Caveats

  • Scaling laws predict loss, not specific capabilities. Some skills seem to appear suddenly, though much of that depends on how they are measured.
  • High-quality data is finite, which pushes interest toward synthetic data and better filtering.
  • Gains now also come from post-training and from test-time compute, where models spend more effort reasoning per question.

Closing Thought

Scaling laws did not explain why large models work, but they made it possible to plan for them. Much of the industry's direction over the past few years follows from those straight lines.
