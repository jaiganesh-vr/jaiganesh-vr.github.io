Why Language Models Hallucinate

Machine Learning Reliability

May 28, 2026

A hallucination is a fluent, confident statement that is false: a paper that was never written, a function that does not exist, a date that is off by a decade. It is tempting to treat these as random glitches, but they follow predictably from how models are built and graded.

The Training Objective

Pre-training rewards producing plausible continuations of text. It does not reward saying "I don't know", because the training data rarely contains that phrase in place of an answer.

For facts that appear often in training data, plausible and true coincide. For rare facts, like a minor person's birthday, the model has learned the shape of the answer without the content, and it fills the shape with something plausible.

The model is not lying. It is completing a pattern where its knowledge runs out.

The Grading Problem

Most benchmarks score answers as right or wrong. A model that guesses when uncertain will sometimes be right; a model that abstains gets zero. Across many questions, guessing scores better.

When models are tuned and selected using such benchmarks, confident guessing is reinforced. Evaluations that give credit for appropriate uncertainty, or penalise confident errors more than abstentions, push in the other direction.

Common Triggers

  • Rare or long-tail facts: names, numbers, citations and niche details.
  • False premises: questions that assume something untrue, which the model goes along with.
  • Pressure to be complete: prompts that demand an answer in a fixed format, with no room for "unknown".
  • Long generations: small errors early on that later text builds upon.

What Actually Helps

  • Grounding: retrieve sources and instruct the model to answer only from them.
  • Permission to abstain: explicitly allow and reward "I don't know" in prompts and evaluations.
  • Citations: require claims to point to a passage, then verify that the passage supports them.
  • Verification: check outputs with tools, such as running code or looking up references.

Newer models hallucinate noticeably less than earlier ones, partly because training now rewards calibrated uncertainty. The problem shrinks but does not disappear.

Closing Thought

Hallucination is best treated as a property to design around, not a bug to wait out. Systems that assume the model will sometimes be confidently wrong are the ones that stay trustworthy.
