Knowledge Distillation

Machine Learning Model Compression

May 04, 2026

Large models are capable but expensive to run. Small models are cheap but weaker. Knowledge distillation is a way to transfer much of a large model's behavior into a small one, so that you pay the training cost once and the inference savings forever.

The Basic Setup

Distillation uses two models:

  • Teacher: a large, accurate model that has already been trained.
  • Student: a smaller model trained to imitate the teacher.

Instead of learning only from the dataset's labels, the student learns from the teacher's outputs.

Why Soft Targets Help

A label says a picture is a cat. A teacher's probabilities say it is 90 percent cat, 8 percent fox and 0.1 percent truck. That extra information, sometimes called dark knowledge, tells the student which mistakes are reasonable and how classes relate.

A probability distribution carries more information per example than a single correct answer.

In the classic formulation, both models' logits are softened with a temperature above 1 before softmax, so that small probabilities become visible, and the student minimises the difference from the teacher's distribution, usually measured with KL divergence. This is often mixed with ordinary loss on the true labels.

Distillation for Language Models

With language models, distillation takes a few forms:

  • Logit distillation: the student matches the teacher's next-token distribution at each position. This needs access to the teacher's probabilities.
  • Sequence distillation: the teacher generates responses, and the student is fine-tuned on them as ordinary training data.
  • Reasoning distillation: the teacher's step-by-step reasoning traces are used to teach a small model to reason in a similar way.

Sequence distillation is the most common, because it only needs the teacher's text output. Many capable small models owe a large share of their quality to training on outputs from larger ones.

Limits

A student rarely surpasses its teacher on the distilled skills, and it inherits the teacher's errors and biases along with its strengths. Small models also have less capacity, so distillation tends to work best on a focused set of tasks rather than everything the teacher can do.

Terms of service matter too: some providers restrict using their model outputs to train competing models.

Closing Thought

Distillation reflects a useful idea: the expensive part of intelligence is often finding the right behavior, not executing it. Once a large model has found it, a smaller one can learn to repeat it.
