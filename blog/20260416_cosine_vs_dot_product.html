Cosine Similarity vs Dot Product

Mathematics Embeddings

Apr 16, 2026

Embedding search ranks documents by how similar their vectors are to a query vector. The two most common measures, dot product and cosine similarity, are closely related, and confusing them is a common source of quiet retrieval bugs.

The Dot Product

For vectors a and b, the dot product multiplies matching components and sums them:

a · b = Σ aᵢ bᵢ = |a| |b| cos θ

The second form shows what it measures: the lengths of both vectors multiplied by the cosine of the angle between them. It grows when vectors point in the same direction and when they are long.

Cosine Similarity

Cosine similarity divides out the lengths:

cos θ = (a · b) / (|a| |b|)

The result depends only on direction and ranges from −1 to 1. Two vectors pointing the same way score 1 regardless of their magnitudes.

When They Agree

If all vectors are normalised to unit length, |a| = |b| = 1, and the dot product equals cosine similarity exactly. Ranking by either gives the same order, and Euclidean distance gives that same order too, since for unit vectors squared distance equals 2 − 2 cos θ.

This is why many embedding models output normalised vectors and why vector databases often recommend dot product for them: it is the cheapest computation that gives the cosine result.

When It Matters

If vectors are not normalised, magnitude enters the score. That can be a feature or a bug:

  • Some models are trained so that magnitude carries meaning, such as confidence or document quality, and dot product uses that signal.
  • Other models produce magnitudes that correlate with text length, so dot product quietly favours long documents.

Use the similarity measure the embedding model was trained with.

Model documentation usually states it. Mixing them, such as indexing with one metric and assuming the other, does not cause errors, only worse results that are hard to trace.

A Note on High Dimensions

In high-dimensional spaces, random vectors are almost always close to orthogonal, so cosine scores between unrelated texts cluster near some baseline rather than at zero. Absolute scores are therefore hard to interpret; rankings and relative gaps are more reliable.

Closing Thought

Similarity metrics are one line of code, but they encode an assumption about what an embedding's length means. Getting that assumption right is a cheap win for retrieval quality.
