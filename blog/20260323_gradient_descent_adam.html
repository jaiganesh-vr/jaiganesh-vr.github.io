Gradient Descent, Momentum and Adam

Mathematics Optimization

Mar 23, 2026

Training a neural network means finding parameter values that make a loss function small. With billions of parameters there is no formula for the answer, so we search for it step by step, guided by the gradient.

Gradient Descent

The gradient of the loss points in the direction of steepest increase. Gradient descent moves the parameters a small step the other way:

θ ← θ − η · ∇L(θ)

The learning rate η controls the step size. Too small and training crawls; too large and the loss oscillates or explodes.

In practice the gradient is estimated on a mini-batch rather than the full dataset. This is stochastic gradient descent. The noise from sampling batches is not just tolerated, it often helps the model avoid poor regions of the loss surface.

Momentum

Loss surfaces are often shaped like long narrow valleys. Plain gradient descent zig-zags across the valley walls while making slow progress along the floor.

Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients and steps in that direction instead. Oscillations across the valley cancel out; consistent movement along it accumulates. The usual decay factor is around 0.9.

Adam

Adam extends momentum by also tracking a running average of squared gradients for every parameter:

  • m: average gradient, the momentum term, decayed with β₁ ≈ 0.9.
  • v: average squared gradient, decayed with β₂ ≈ 0.999.
  • Update: step by m divided by the square root of v, plus a small ε.

Dividing by √v gives each parameter its own effective learning rate. Parameters with consistently large gradients take smaller steps; parameters with small or rare gradients take larger ones. Both averages are bias-corrected because they start at zero.

Adam's real contribution is per-parameter step sizes, which makes training far less sensitive to tuning.

AdamW and Schedules

AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient update, which fixed a subtle interaction in the original algorithm and became the default for training transformers.

Large models also use learning rate schedules: a short warm-up from near zero, then a gradual decay, often following a cosine curve. Warm-up matters because Adam's statistics are unreliable in the first steps.

Closing Thought

The optimizer is easy to overlook because it rarely changes, but every capability a model has was found one small step at a time by this process.
