The Sampling Distribution of an Estimator

Machine Learning Statistics

Oct 08, 2022

Fit a model to a dataset and you get one set of numbers. Had a slightly different dataset come along, you would have got different numbers. The sampling distribution describes that variation, and it is the frequentist foundation for talking about uncertainty.

The Idea

An estimator is a function of the data, θ̂ = t(D). Data is random, so θ̂ is random too. Imagine drawing many datasets of the same size from the true distribution and computing θ̂ on each. The distribution of the results is the sampling distribution.

It is a distribution over answers to a question we asked just once.

Bias and Variance

Two numbers summarise it:

  • Bias: E[θ̂] − θ, how far the estimator is from the truth on average.
  • Variance: E[(θ̂ − E[θ̂])²], how much it moves from one dataset to the next.

Their combination gives the mean squared error:

MSE = bias² + variance

The sample variance shows why bias matters. Dividing by n underestimates σ² on average, because deviations are measured from the sample mean, which sits closer to the data than the true mean does. The expectation works out to (n − 1)/n · σ², so dividing by n − 1 removes the bias.

An unbiased estimator is not automatically the best one. Accepting a small bias for a large reduction in variance can lower the MSE, which is what regularisation does.

The Sample Mean

The sample mean is the case with a clean result:

Var(x̄) = σ² / n, so the standard error is σ / √n

To halve the standard error, you need four times as much data. By the central limit theorem, its sampling distribution is approximately Gaussian for large n, whatever the distribution of the data.

Confidence Intervals

An approximate 95% interval is θ̂ ± 1.96 · SE. The interpretation is subtle: if we repeated the whole procedure many times, about 95% of the intervals would contain θ. It does not say there is a 95% probability that θ lies in this particular interval, which is the statement a Bayesian credible interval makes.

The Bootstrap

For most estimators, such as a median, a correlation or a test accuracy, no formula exists. We also cannot draw new datasets from the true distribution. The bootstrap uses the data we have as a stand-in for it:

  • Draw n points from the data, with replacement.
  • Compute the estimate on this resampled dataset.
  • Repeat a few thousand times, and treat the results as an approximate sampling distribution.
import numpy as np

def bootstrap_se(data, estimator, n_boot=2000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = len(data)
    stats = [estimator(rng.choice(data, n, replace=True)) for _ in range(n_boot)]
    return np.std(stats, ddof=1)

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
data = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=200)

print(bootstrap_se(data, np.mean))    # close to the formula below
print(data.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(200))
print(bootstrap_se(data, np.median))  # no simple formula needed

Each resample contains about 63% of the distinct original points, since each point is missed with probability (1 − 1/n)ⁿ ≈ 1/e. Bagging in random forests relies on this same resampling.

The bootstrap has limits. It does poorly for statistics driven by extremes, such as the maximum, and it cannot fix a dataset that is unrepresentative to begin with.

Frequentist and Bayesian Uncertainty

The two views ask different questions. The frequentist holds θ fixed and asks how the estimate would vary across datasets. The Bayesian holds the data fixed and asks how θ is distributed given it. With plenty of data and a weak prior, the answers often agree numerically, although their meanings differ.

Closing Thought

Every metric on a dashboard and every fitted weight comes from a sampling distribution we usually see only one draw of. Keeping this in mind makes it easier to tell a real improvement from noise.
