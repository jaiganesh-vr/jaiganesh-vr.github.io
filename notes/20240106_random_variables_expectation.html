Random Variables, Expectation and Variance

Mathematics Probability

Jan 06, 2024

Machine learning is full of quantities that vary: data, noise, mini-batch gradients, weight initialisation. Random variables give a precise language for this, and two summary numbers, expectation and variance, handle a surprising amount of the theory.

Random Variables

A random variable assigns a number to each outcome of a random process. Discrete ones have a probability mass function, which sums to one. Continuous ones have a probability density function, which integrates to one:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ p(x) dx

A density is not a probability. It can exceed one, as long as the total area is one. A uniform distribution on [0, 0.5] has density 2.

Expectation

Expectation is the probability-weighted average:

E[X] = Σ x · P(x) or ∫ x · p(x) dx

Its most useful property is linearity, which holds with no independence assumption at all:

E[aX + bY] = a E[X] + b E[Y]

Linearity of expectation works even when the variables are entangled. Many difficult calculations reduce to this.

As an example, what is the expected number of people out of n who get their own hat back when hats are returned at random? Each one has a 1/n chance, so the expectation is n · (1/n) = 1, even though the events are all dependent.

Functions must be handled with care, since E[f(X)] is usually not f(E[X]). For convex f, Jensen's inequality gives:

E[f(X)] ≥ f(E[X])

This inequality is behind the EM lower bound and the fact that KL divergence is never negative.

Variance

Variance measures the expected squared distance from the mean:

Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²] = E[X²] − E[X]²

Scaling behaves differently than for expectation, because squaring brings out the constant:

Var(aX + b) = a² Var(X)

Covariance and Correlation

Covariance measures how two variables move together, and correlation rescales it to lie between −1 and 1:

Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − E[X])(Y − E[Y])]

Variance of a sum needs this term:

Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y)

Independent variables have zero covariance, but the reverse is false. If X is symmetric around zero and Y = X², they have no correlation, and yet Y is completely determined by X. Correlation only sees linear association.

Averaging Reduces Noise

For the mean of n independent variables with variance σ²:

Var(x̄) = σ² / n

Applied to correlated variables, the same calculation shows that averaging helps less. This fact underlies mini-batch training, where batch gradients become less noisy with batch size, and also ensembles, which do better the less their members are correlated. 

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(0, 2, (100_000, 16))   # variance 4

print(x[:, 0].var())                  # about 4
print(x.mean(axis=1).var())           # about 4 / 16 = 0.25

# Uncorrelated but dependent
a = rng.normal(0, 1, 100_000)
print(np.corrcoef(a, a**2)[0, 1])     # about 0

Conditional Expectation

E[Y | X] is itself a random variable, a function of X. It is the best predictor of Y in squared error, which is why a regression trained on squared loss is trying to estimate it. Two identities are often helpful:

  • Tower rule: E[Y] = E[ E[Y | X] ]
  • Total variance: Var(Y) = E[ Var(Y | X) ] + Var( E[Y | X] )

The second splits variance into the part a predictor cannot remove and the part it can capture.

Closing Thought

Expectation and variance do a lot with little. They are enough to compute how estimates behave long before we say what distribution is involved. The most important of those distributions is the Gaussian.
