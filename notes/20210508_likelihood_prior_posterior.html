Likelihood, Prior and Posterior

Machine Learning Bayesian Inference

May 08, 2021

Bayesian machine learning treats unknown parameters the way we treat any uncertain quantity: with probability. Three terms do almost all of the work, and most confusion in the subject comes from mixing them up.

The Setup

We have data D and a model with parameters θ. Bayes' rule connects what we believed before seeing the data to what we should believe afterwards:

P(θ | D) = P(D | θ) · P(θ) / P(D)

  • Prior, P(θ): what we believe about θ before seeing any data.
  • Likelihood, P(D | θ): how probable the observed data would be if θ were the true value.
  • Posterior, P(θ | D): what we believe about θ after seeing the data.
  • Evidence, P(D): the probability of the data averaged over all θ. It rescales the posterior so it sums to one.

Likelihood Is Not a Probability of θ

This is the most common mistake. Viewed as a function of D with θ fixed, P(D | θ) is a probability distribution. Viewed as a function of θ with D fixed, it is not. It need not integrate to one over θ, and it says nothing about which θ is probable until a prior is brought in.

The likelihood scores parameters by how well they explain the data. It takes a prior to turn those scores into beliefs.

A Worked Example

Suppose a coin lands heads 7 times in 10 flips, and θ is its probability of heads. The likelihood is:

L(θ) = θ⁷ · (1 − θ)³

It peaks at θ = 0.7, which is what the data alone suggests. Now add a mild belief that the coin is roughly fair, using a Beta(2, 2) prior:

P(θ) ∝ θ · (1 − θ)

Multiplying prior and likelihood gives the posterior, which is another Beta distribution:

P(θ | D) ∝ θ⁸ · (1 − θ)⁴, which is Beta(9, 5)

The posterior mean is 9 / 14 ≈ 0.643, pulled from 0.7 toward the prior's 0.5. With 700 heads in 1,000 flips the pull almost disappears, because the data outweighs the prior. 

import numpy as np

theta = np.linspace(0, 1, 1001)
prior = theta * (1 - theta)
likelihood = theta**7 * (1 - theta)**3

posterior = prior * likelihood
posterior /= posterior.sum()   # the evidence, up to the grid spacing

print((theta * posterior).sum())   # 0.643

Conjugate Priors

The posterior above stayed in the Beta family because the Beta prior is conjugate to the coin-flip likelihood. A Beta(a, b) prior behaves like a heads and b tails seen in advance, and updating is just adding counts. Conjugacy is convenient but limited to simple models. Most real posteriors need approximation.

Using the Posterior

Keeping a full distribution instead of a single estimate pays off in several ways:

  • Uncertainty: the spread of the posterior says how much the data has actually pinned θ down.
  • Prediction: the posterior predictive averages over θ, P(x | D) = ∫ P(x | θ) P(θ | D) dθ, instead of trusting one value.
  • Sequential updating: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior, so learning from data in batches gives the same answer as learning from it all at once.

Closing Thought

Prior, likelihood and posterior separate what we assumed, what the data says and what we conclude. The next question is what to do when a single best value is all we can afford, which leads to maximum likelihood and MAP estimation.
