The Hessian, Taylor Series and Newton's Method

Mathematics Calculus

Jul 22, 2023

A gradient tells us which way is downhill. It does not tell us how quickly the slope changes, which determines how big a step is safe. For that we need second derivatives.

Taylor Series

A smooth function can be approximated near a point by a polynomial that matches its derivatives there. In one dimension:

f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + f′(x) h + ½ f″(x) h² + …

For several variables, the quadratic approximation is:

f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + ∇f(x)ᵀh + ½ hᵀ H h

Each extra term improves accuracy near x, while none of them promise much far away.

The Hessian

H is the matrix of all second partial derivatives, Hᵢⱼ = ∂²f / ∂xᵢ∂xⱼ. For smooth functions it is symmetric. Its eigenvectors are the principal directions of curvature, and its eigenvalues give the curvature along each.

The ratio between the largest and smallest eigenvalue is the condition number. When this is large, the surface looks like a long narrow valley. Gradient descent then zigzags across the steep walls and moves slowly along the floor. To stay stable, its step size must satisfy η < 2/λ_max, and that restriction slows the flat directions.

Classifying Stationary Points

At a point where the gradient is zero, the quadratic term decides what happens:

  • All eigenvalues positive: a local minimum.
  • All eigenvalues negative: a local maximum.
  • Mixed signs: a saddle point.
  • Some eigenvalues zero: this test can't decide.

For f(x, y) = x² − y², the origin has gradient zero and eigenvalues 2 and −2, so it is a saddle. In high dimensions, random stationary points are much more likely to be saddles than minima, since this requires only one negative eigenvalue among millions.

Newton's Method

Rather than stepping along the gradient by an arbitrary amount, jump to the minimum of the local quadratic model. Setting the gradient of the quadratic to zero gives:

h = − H⁻¹ ∇f(x)

Newton's method rescales each direction by its curvature, turning a narrow valley into a round bowl.

On an exactly quadratic function, it reaches the minimum in one step. Near a solution, it converges quadratically, which means the number of correct digits roughly doubles with every step. 

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[100.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]])   # condition number 100

def grad(x):
    return A @ x

x_gd = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
for step in range(100):
    x_gd = x_gd - 0.019 * grad(x_gd)       # just under 2 / 100

x_newton = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
x_newton = x_newton - np.linalg.solve(A, grad(x_newton))

print(x_gd.round(4))       # flat direction is still far from zero
print(x_newton)            # exactly zero after one step

Why Deep Learning Rarely Uses It

  • Cost: storing H takes O(d²) memory, and solving with it takes O(d³) time. This is out of the question for a model with millions of parameters.
  • Saddle points: on non-convex functions, the pure Newton step moves toward any stationary point, including saddles and maxima.
  • Noise: Hessians estimated from mini-batches are unreliable.

Practical Middle Ground

  • Quasi-Newton: BFGS and L-BFGS build an approximation of the inverse Hessian from recent gradients.
  • Hessian-vector products: Hv can be computed at about the cost of one or two gradients, without forming H.
  • Damping: using (H + λI)⁻¹ keeps a Newton step safe by interpolating toward gradient descent.
  • Diagonal scaling: Adam's per-parameter step sizes can be loosely thought of as a cheap curvature correction.

Closing Thought

First derivatives give direction, and second derivatives say how to scale it. Much of practical optimisation is a search for ways of capturing curvature's benefit without paying its full cost.
