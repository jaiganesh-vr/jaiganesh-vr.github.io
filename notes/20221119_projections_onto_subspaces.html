Projections Onto Subspaces

Mathematics Linear Algebra

Nov 19, 2022

When Ax = b has no solution, because b lies outside the column space of A, the sensible question is what comes closest. The answer is a projection, and the calculation is exactly what underlies least squares.

The Geometry

Let the columns of A be a basis for a subspace. We want the point p = Ax̂ in that subspace that is closest to b. The closest point is where the error e = b − p is perpendicular to the subspace. Any other point is further away, by Pythagoras.

The Normal Equations

Being perpendicular to the subspace means being perpendicular to each column of A:

Aᵀ(b − Ax̂) = 0

Rearranging this gives the normal equations:

AᵀA x̂ = Aᵀb

Independent columns make AᵀA invertible, so:

x̂ = (AᵀA)⁻¹ Aᵀb and p = A (AᵀA)⁻¹ Aᵀ b

The Projection Matrix

The matrix that takes b to p is:

P = A (AᵀA)⁻¹ Aᵀ

It depends only on the subspace, not on the particular basis chosen. Note that the formula cannot be simplified by splitting up (AᵀA)⁻¹, because A is not square in general. Two properties characterise projection matrices:

  • Symmetric: Pᵀ = P.
  • Idempotent: P² = P, since projecting something already in the subspace leaves it where it is.

Project once and you are in the subspace. Project again and nothing moves.

The matrix I − P is also a projection, onto the orthogonal complement. So every vector splits cleanly in two: b = Pb + (I − P)b, with a part in the column space and a part in the left null space.

If the columns are orthonormal, the formula collapses to P = QQᵀ, and then p = Σ (qᵢᵀb) qᵢ, with each direction handled independently. This is one of the main reasons for computing a QR factorisation.

Fitting a Line

Find the best line y = C + Dt through the points (0, 6), (1, 0) and (2, 0). Forcing the line through all of the points gives three equations in two unknowns:

[[1, 0], [1, 1], [1, 2]] · (C, D) = (6, 0, 0)

There is no exact solution. The normal equations are:

3C + 3D = 6 and 3C + 5D = 0

Solving gives C = 5 and D = −3, so the best line is y = 5 − 3t. Its values at the three times are p = (5, 2, −1), and the errors are e = (1, −2, 1). The error is perpendicular to both columns, as it should be: 1 − 2 + 1 = 0 and 0 − 2 + 2 = 0.

Minimising the sum of squared errors with calculus gives the same equations. So the algebra, the geometry and the calculus are all describing one calculation. 

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0]])
b = np.array([6.0, 0.0, 0.0])

x_hat = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ b)
P = A @ np.linalg.inv(A.T @ A) @ A.T
e = b - P @ b

print(x_hat)                          # [ 5. -3.]
print(e.round(10))                    # [ 1. -2.  1.]
print((A.T @ e).round(10))            # [0. 0.]: perpendicular to the columns
print(np.allclose(P @ P, P))          # True
print(round(np.trace(P), 10))         # 2.0: the dimension of the subspace

Practical Notes

  • Do not form AᵀA in practice: this squares the condition number. Library routines like numpy.linalg.lstsq use QR or the SVD.
  • Do not form P either: the matrix is m × m and usually dense, while computing p directly takes far less work.
  • Eigenvalues: vectors in the subspace have eigenvalue 1 and vectors perpendicular to it have eigenvalue 0. So the trace of P equals the dimension of the subspace.
  • Outliers: squaring makes large errors dominate, so a single bad point can noticeably tilt the fit.

Closing Thought

Projection takes an equation that cannot be solved and replaces it with the nearest one that can. Many statistics and signal processing procedures come down to choosing a subspace and dropping a perpendicular onto it.
