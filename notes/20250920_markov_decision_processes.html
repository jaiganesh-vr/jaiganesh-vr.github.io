Markov Decision Processes

Artificial Intelligence Decisions

Sep 20, 2025

One-shot decisions pick a single action. Many problems require a sequence of decisions, where each action affects future options and the outcomes are uncertain. Markov decision processes formalise that.

The Components

An MDP is defined by:

  • States S: the situations the agent can be in.
  • Actions A(s): what the agent can do in each state.
  • Transition model P(s' | s, a): the probability of reaching s' by doing a in s.
  • Reward function R(s, a, s'): the immediate reward for each transition.
  • Discount factor γ: between 0 and 1, how much future rewards count.

The Markov property means transitions depend only on the current state and action, not on the history.

A Grid World

The classic example is a robot in a small grid. Each move succeeds with probability 0.8 and slips sideways otherwise. Two exit squares give +1 and −1, and every other step costs a little. The robot must find a route to +1 that avoids the risk of slipping into −1.

Policies

Because outcomes are uncertain, a fixed sequence of actions is not enough. The solution is a policy π(s), which specifies an action for every state. An optimal policy maximises expected total discounted reward.

A plan says what to do next. A policy says what to do wherever you end up.

The best policy depends on the rewards. If each step is very costly, the robot risks the dangerous path. If steps are cheap, it takes the long, safe route.

Discounting

The total value of a sequence of rewards is:

r₀ + γ r₁ + γ² r₂ + …

Discounting keeps total reward finite in infinite tasks, and reflects a preference for sooner rewards. A γ close to 1 makes the agent far-sighted; close to 0, myopic.

The Bellman Equation

The utility of a state is the immediate reward plus the discounted utility of the best next state, in expectation:

U(s) = max over a of Σ P(s' | s, a) [ R(s, a, s') + γ U(s') ]

This recursive relationship, the Bellman equation, defines the optimal utilities. Once they are known, the optimal policy simply chooses the action that achieves the maximum in each state.

Partially Observable MDPs

When the agent cannot observe the state directly, the problem becomes a POMDP. The agent acts on a belief state, a probability distribution over states, combining the ideas of HMMs and MDPs. POMDPs are far harder to solve exactly.
