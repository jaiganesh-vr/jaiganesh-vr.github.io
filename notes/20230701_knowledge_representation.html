Knowledge Representation

Artificial Intelligence Knowledge

Jul 01, 2023

Logic provides a language. Knowledge representation is about deciding what to say in it: which concepts to define, how to organise them, and how to capture the messy, general knowledge that intelligent behavior relies on.

Ontologies

An ontology is a structured vocabulary of concepts and their relationships. An upper ontology captures the most general concepts: objects, events, time, space, substances, mental states. Domain ontologies add concepts for specific fields, like medicine or law.

Categories and Objects

Much reasoning happens at the level of categories rather than individual objects. Knowing something is a basketball tells you it is round and bounces.

  • Membership: BB₉ ∈ Basketballs.
  • Subclass: Basketballs ⊂ Balls.
  • Inheritance: properties of a category apply to its members and subcategories.

These form taxonomies, hierarchies that organise vast amounts of knowledge compactly. Biology's classification of species is one; product catalogues are another.

Events and Time

Reasoning about change requires representing actions and their effects over time. The event calculus represents events and fluents, facts that change, with predicates like Happens(e, t) and HoldsAt(f, t). This allows reasoning about what is true at any moment.

Semantic Networks

Semantic networks draw knowledge as graphs: nodes for objects and categories, labelled links for relations like IsA and HasPart. They make inheritance easy to visualise and compute. Modern knowledge graphs, which power search engine fact panels, are descendants of this idea.

A knowledge graph is a semantic network at web scale.

Description Logics

Description logics are carefully restricted logics for defining and reasoning about categories. They trade expressiveness for guaranteed efficient inference, answering questions like whether one category is a subset of another. The Web Ontology Language, OWL, is based on them.

Default Reasoning

Much common knowledge has exceptions. Birds fly, except penguins and ostriches. Classical logic is monotonic: adding facts never removes conclusions. Default reasoning is non-monotonic: learning that Tweety is a penguin should retract the conclusion that Tweety flies.

Approaches include default logic, circumscription and truth maintenance systems, which track why each belief is held so that it can be revised.

The Knowledge Bottleneck

Encoding knowledge by hand turned out to be slow and brittle. That difficulty is one reason the field shifted toward learning knowledge from data, though structured representations remain important wherever precision and explanation matter.

Closing Thought

Knowledge representation asks what an intelligent system needs to know and how to organise it. Those questions remain relevant even when the knowledge is learned rather than written.
