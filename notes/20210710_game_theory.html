Game Theory Basics

Artificial Intelligence Game Theory

Jul 10, 2021

Adversarial search handles turn-taking games with perfect information. Game theory is broader: it studies any situation where several rational agents make decisions whose outcomes depend on each other's choices, often simultaneously.

Normal-Form Games

A game in normal form specifies:

  • Players: the decision-makers.
  • Actions: the strategies available to each player.
  • Payoffs: a utility for each player, for every combination of actions.

The Prisoner's Dilemma

Two suspects are questioned separately. Each can testify against the other or refuse. If both refuse, each gets 1 year. If both testify, each gets 5 years. If only one testifies, that one goes free and the other gets 10 years.

Whatever the other suspect does, testifying is better for you: free instead of 1 year, or 5 years instead of 10. Testifying is a dominant strategy, so both testify and each serves 5 years, even though both refusing would have been better for both.

Individually rational choices can produce a collectively worse outcome.

Dominant Strategies

A strategy strictly dominates another if it gives a better payoff regardless of what others do. A rational player never plays a dominated strategy, and iteratively eliminating them can sometimes solve a game.

Nash Equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of strategies where no player can gain by changing their own strategy alone. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, both testifying is the equilibrium.

Some games have no equilibrium in pure strategies. In rock-paper-scissors any fixed choice can be exploited. The equilibrium is a mixed strategy: play each option with probability 1/3. Nash proved that every finite game has at least one equilibrium in mixed strategies.

Pareto Optimality

An outcome is Pareto optimal if no other outcome makes someone better off without making someone worse off. The Prisoner's Dilemma equilibrium is not Pareto optimal, which is precisely what makes it a dilemma.

Repeated Games

When the same game is played many times, cooperation can emerge. Strategies like tit-for-tat, which cooperates first and then copies the opponent's last move, do well because they reward cooperation and punish defection.

Mechanism Design

The inverse problem is designing the rules of a game so that rational agents produce a desired outcome. Auctions are the classic example: a second-price sealed-bid auction makes bidding your true value the dominant strategy.

Closing Thought

Game theory matters for AI whenever several agents share an environment, from trading systems to negotiating software agents. It shows that rationality alone does not guarantee good collective results.
