Genetic Algorithms

Artificial Intelligence Optimization

Aug 01, 2020

Genetic algorithms are a family of local search methods inspired by evolution. Instead of improving one state, they maintain a population of candidate solutions and combine the good ones to produce new candidates.

The Ingredients

  • Representation: each individual is encoded as a string, often of bits or digits. For 8-queens, a string of eight digits giving each queen's row.
  • Fitness function: scores how good each individual is. For 8-queens, the number of non-attacking pairs.
  • Population: a set of individuals, usually initialised randomly.

One Generation

  • Selection: choose parents with probability proportional to fitness, so better individuals reproduce more often.
  • Crossover: pick a crossover point and swap the parents' strings after it, producing two children.
  • Mutation: with small probability, change random positions in each child.
  • Replacement: the children form the next generation.

Repeat until an individual is fit enough or a time limit is reached.

Why Crossover Matters

Mutation alone is just a random walk. Crossover is what makes genetic algorithms different: it can combine large, independently evolved good blocks from two parents into one child.

Crossover helps when good solutions are built from good parts that can be combined.

This only works when the encoding places related components together. If useful blocks are scattered randomly across the string, crossover mostly destroys them.

Schemas

The theory behind this uses schemas: patterns where some positions are fixed and others are free, such as strings starting with 246 followed by any five digits. Schemas whose instances have above-average fitness tend to increase in the population over generations, so good partial solutions spread.

Strengths and Weaknesses

  • Strengths: need little problem knowledge, work in rugged landscapes and parallelise naturally.
  • Weaknesses: many parameters to tune (population size, mutation rate, selection method), no guarantee of optimality, and often slower than a well-designed specific method.

Genetic algorithms have been used for scheduling, circuit design and evolving neural network architectures. Related methods, such as genetic programming, evolve programs rather than fixed strings.

Closing Thought

Evolution is a slow but general optimizer. Genetic algorithms borrow that generality, and are most useful when a problem is poorly understood but candidates are easy to evaluate.
