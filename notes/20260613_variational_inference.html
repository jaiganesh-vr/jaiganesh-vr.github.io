Variational Inference

Machine Learning Approximate Inference

Jun 13, 2026

MCMC approximates a posterior with samples, and it can take a long time. Variational inference changes the question. Rather than sampling from the posterior, find the member of a simple family of distributions that is closest to it. Inference becomes optimisation, with all of optimisation's speed and tooling.

The Objective

Choose a family q(z; φ), such as Gaussians with diagonal covariance, and minimise the KL divergence to the posterior:

φ* = argmin KL( q(z; φ) ‖ p(z | x) )

This can't be computed directly since it contains the very evidence we cannot calculate. But it can be rearranged:

log p(x) = ELBO(φ) + KL( q(z; φ) ‖ p(z | x) )

ELBO(φ) = E_q[ log p(x, z) − log q(z) ]

The ELBO needs only the joint, which we can evaluate. And since log p(x) does not depend on φ, maximising the ELBO minimises the KL. Since the KL is never negative, the ELBO is also a lower bound on the evidence, hence its name.

Reading the ELBO

Rewriting it shows the two forces at work:

ELBO = E_q[ log p(x | z) ] − KL( q(z) ‖ p(z) )

The first term rewards explaining the data. The second keeps q close to the prior. This is the same fit-against-complexity balance seen across Bayesian methods.

EM performs this same maximisation, with q set to the exact posterior. Variational inference is what happens when that exact posterior is out of reach.

Mean Field

The classic family assumes that q factorises over the latent variables:

q(z) = Π qⱼ(zⱼ)

Holding the other factors fixed, the best update for one has a closed form:

log qⱼ*(zⱼ) = E₋ⱼ[ log p(x, z) ] + const

Coordinate ascent cycles through these updates and the ELBO rises at each one. For conditionally conjugate models, the updates are straightforward to write down. Latent Dirichlet allocation was a famous early application.

Stochastic Gradients

Modern variational inference works for far more general models by estimating gradients of the ELBO with samples. With data subsampling, it can scale to very large datasets. Two estimators dominate:

  • Reparameterisation: write z = μ + σ·ε with ε ~ N(0, I), so gradients flow through the sample. This has low variance, but z must be continuous.
  • Score function (REINFORCE): uses ∇ log q and works for discrete z, but has high variance and usually needs control variates.

Amortised Inference and VAEs

Fitting separate variational parameters for every data point is expensive. Amortised inference trains a neural network, the encoder, that maps x directly to q(z | x). Combined with a neural decoder p(x | z), and trained on the ELBO with reparameterisation, this is the variational autoencoder.

What the Speed Costs

Minimising KL(q ‖ p) heavily penalises q for placing mass where p has little, but it doesn't mind q missing parts of p. This has predictable consequences:

  • Underestimated variance: q tends to be too narrow, so uncertainty looks smaller than it is.
  • Missed modes: a unimodal q settles on one mode and ignores the others.
  • Mean field blind spots: factorising removes posterior correlations by construction.
  • No guarantee of convergence to the truth: unlike MCMC, more compute does not fix a bad family. The gap in the bound remains.

Richer families, such as normalising flows, can narrow this gap at some additional cost.

Variational Inference or MCMC

  • Variational inference: when you have large data or models, need many repeated fits, and can accept approximation.
  • MCMC: when accurate uncertainty matters more than speed, and the model is small enough.

A useful check is to compare both on a subset of the data. This shows how far the approximation drifts.

Closing Thought

Variational inference changes the question from "what is the posterior?" to "what is the closest thing I can afford?". It is honest about being an approximation, and it is the reason Bayesian ideas now reach into deep learning.
