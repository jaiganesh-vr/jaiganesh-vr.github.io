Taylor's Series

Mathematics Calculus

Mar 28, 2026

Polynomials are the simplest functions there are, requiring only addition and multiplication. Taylor's series show that many other functions, including eˣ, sin x and ln x, are polynomials of infinite degree in disguise.

Finding the Coefficients

Suppose f can be written as a power series around 0:

f(x) = a₀ + a₁x + a₂x² + a₃x³ + …

Setting x = 0 gives a₀ = f(0). Differentiating once and setting x = 0 gives a₁ = f′(0). Differentiating twice gives 2a₂ = f″(0). In general, differentiating n times brings out a factor of n! on aₙ. So the coefficients are determined by the derivatives at a single point:

aₙ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(0) / n!

This gives the Taylor series around a point a, whose special case at 0 is often called a Maclaurin series:

f(x) = Σₙ f⁽ⁿ⁾(a) / n! · (x − a)ⁿ

All the information about the function is contained in its derivatives at a single point, when the series converges to it.

The Standard Series

A few series around 0 are worth knowing by heart:

  • Exponential: eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + …
  • Sine: sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − …
  • Cosine: cos x = 1 − x²/2! + x⁴/4! − …
  • Geometric: 1 / (1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + x³ + …, for |x| < 1.
  • Logarithm: ln(1 + x) = x − x²/2 + x³/3 − …, for −1 < x ≤ 1.

Substituting x = iθ into the exponential series and separating the real and imaginary parts gives Euler's formula, e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ, which unifies the first three of these.

Radius of Convergence

A power series converges on an interval centred at a, of radius R, and diverges beyond it. The ratio test finds R, by comparing successive terms:

R = lim |aₙ / aₙ₊₁|

For eˣ the ratio is n + 1, so R is infinite. For the geometric series, R = 1.

Why does 1 / (1 + x²), a function that is perfectly smooth on the real line, have a series that stops working at |x| = 1? The reason lies in the complex plane, where the function blows up at x = ±i. Those points are distance 1 from the origin, and they limit the radius.

The Remainder

Stopping after degree n leaves an error, and Lagrange's form of it looks much like the next term, with the derivative evaluated at some unknown point c between a and x:

Rₙ(x) = f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(c) / (n + 1)! · (x − a)ⁿ⁺¹

This is a generalisation of the mean value theorem, which is the case n = 0. A bound on the derivative gives a bound on the error. For eˣ on [0, 1], the error from stopping at degree n is at most e / (n + 1)!, so only a handful of terms give high accuracy. 

import math

def exp_taylor(x, n):
    return sum(x**k / math.factorial(k) for k in range(n + 1))

def log1p_taylor(x, n):
    return sum((-1)**(k + 1) * x**k / k for k in range(1, n + 1))

for n in (2, 5, 10, 15):
    error = abs(exp_taylor(1, n) - math.e)
    bound = math.e / math.factorial(n + 1)
    print(n, f"{error:.2e}", f"{bound:.2e}")

# ln 2 at the edge of the interval converges slowly
for n in (10, 1000):
    print(n, f"{abs(log1p_taylor(1, n) - math.log(2)):.2e}")

The speed varies widely. Each term of eˣ shrinks factorially, whereas ln 2 from the logarithm series gains about one correct digit each time the number of terms goes up tenfold.

Working With Series

There is rarely any need to differentiate many times. Inside its radius, a series can be manipulated term by term:

  • Substitute: e^(−x²) = 1 − x² + x⁴/2! − …, which provides a way of integrating this function.
  • Differentiate: differentiating the sine series term by term gives the cosine series.
  • Integrate: integrating 1 / (1 + x²) = 1 − x² + x⁴ − … gives arctan x = x − x³/3 + x⁵/5 − ….
  • Multiply: multiply series like polynomials, keeping only the terms needed.

Setting x = 1 in the arctan series gives a famous formula: π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …. It converges too slowly to be practical, but it is a striking link between odd numbers and circles.

Limits and Approximations

Series make many limits immediate. (1 − cos x) / x² is (x²/2 − x⁴/24 + …) / x², which tends to 1/2, with no need for L'Hôpital's rule. They also justify small-angle approximations like sin x ≈ x, which is accurate to about 0.2% at x = 0.1 radians and improves quickly for smaller angles.

A Warning

Convergence of a Taylor series does not guarantee that it converges to the function. The standard counterexample is:

f(x) = e^(−1/x²), with f(0) = 0

It is smooth, and every derivative at 0 is zero. Its Taylor series is identically zero, yet the function is not. Functions that do equal their Taylor series nearby are called analytic. Most of the functions we meet in applications are analytic, but the condition is not automatic.

Closing Thought

Taylor's series turn functions into a list of numbers, and let hard operations become arithmetic on those numbers. Much of numerical computing relies on this idea: replace a difficult function with a polynomial that is close enough.
