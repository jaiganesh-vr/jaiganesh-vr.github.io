Inference in Propositional Logic

Artificial Intelligence Logic

Sep 24, 2022

Model checking enumerates every possible world, which quickly becomes infeasible. Theorem proving instead applies inference rules directly to sentences, deriving new sentences without considering models at all.

Soundness and Completeness

  • An inference procedure is sound if everything it derives is actually entailed.
  • It is complete if it can derive everything that is entailed.

Soundness is essential; an unsound procedure can prove falsehoods.

Basic Inference Rules

  • Modus ponens: from P ⇒ Q and P, infer Q.
  • And-elimination: from P ∧ Q, infer P.
  • Logical equivalences: such as De Morgan's laws and contraposition, which can be applied in either direction.

Resolution

Resolution is a single inference rule that is complete for propositional logic when used in refutation. It works on clauses, disjunctions of literals:

From (A ∨ B) and (¬B ∨ C), infer (A ∨ C)

The complementary literals B and ¬B cancel out.

To prove KB ⊨ α, convert KB ∧ ¬α into conjunctive normal form, a conjunction of clauses, and apply resolution repeatedly. If the empty clause appears, the set is unsatisfiable and α is proved.

Resolution proves things by showing that their negation leads to a contradiction.

Horn Clauses and Chaining

Many knowledge bases use Horn clauses, which have at most one positive literal, like (A ∧ B) ⇒ C. For these, inference is much more efficient:

  • Forward chaining: start from known facts, fire every rule whose premises are satisfied, add conclusions, and repeat. It is data-driven and runs in linear time.
  • Backward chaining: start from the query and work backward, looking for rules that conclude it and trying to prove their premises. It is goal-driven and only touches relevant facts.

Forward chaining suits agents updating beliefs from new percepts. Backward chaining suits answering specific questions.

SAT Solvers

Checking satisfiability of a general formula is NP-complete, yet modern SAT solvers routinely handle problems with millions of variables. They build on the DPLL algorithm, a backtracking search with:

  • Unit propagation: if a clause has only one unassigned literal, it must be true.
  • Pure symbols: a symbol appearing with only one sign can be set to satisfy all its clauses.
  • Clause learning: conflicts produce new clauses that prevent repeating the same mistake.

Local search methods like WalkSAT also work well on many satisfiable instances.

Closing Thought

Logical inference turns a knowledge base into a source of new conclusions. The progress of SAT solvers shows that worst-case hardness does not always mean practical hardness.
