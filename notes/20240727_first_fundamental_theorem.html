The First Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Mathematics Calculus

Jul 27, 2024

Area under a curve is defined by an infinite limit of tiny rectangles, which looks like it would be hopeless to compute. Yet for many functions, you need only two values of an antiderivative. This surprising connection between the two halves of calculus is the first fundamental theorem.

Textbooks differ in which part of the theorem they call first. Here, the first theorem is the evaluation formula, and the second theorem is the derivative of an integral.

The Definite Integral

Divide [a, b] into n pieces of width Δx. Choose a point cᵢ in each piece, and add the areas of the rectangles:

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = lim (n → ∞) Σ f(cᵢ) Δx

This counts area below the x-axis as negative. So it measures net signed area. For example, the integral of sin x over [0, 2π] is zero.

The Theorem

If f is continuous on [a, b] and F is any antiderivative of f, so that F′ = f, then:

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

A limit of infinitely many terms collapses to two values.

Which antiderivative you choose does not matter, because adding a constant C cancels in the subtraction.

For example, F(x) = x³/3 is an antiderivative of x²:

∫₀¹ x² dx = 1/3 − 0 = 1/3

And F(x) = −cos x is an antiderivative of sin x:

∫₀^π sin x dx = −cos π + cos 0 = 2

So the area under one arch of the sine wave is exactly 2, a fact that is not at all obvious from its picture.

A Short Proof

The mean value theorem provides an elegant argument. Cut [a, b] at points x₀, x₁, …, xₙ. The total change in F is the sum of the changes on each piece:

F(b) − F(a) = Σ [ F(xᵢ) − F(xᵢ₋₁) ]

The mean value theorem says each difference equals F′(cᵢ) Δxᵢ for some cᵢ in that piece. Since F′ = f:

F(b) − F(a) = Σ f(cᵢ) Δxᵢ

This is a Riemann sum, with special sample points. The left side does not depend on n, and the right side goes to the integral. So they are equal.

Accumulated Change

Another reading is that adding up a rate of change gives total change:

∫ₐᵇ F′(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

Integrate velocity to get displacement, not distance, because backward motion counts as negative. Integrating a flow rate gives the total amount accumulated. 

import numpy as np

def midpoint(f, a, b, n):
    dx = (b - a) / n
    return (f(a + dx * (np.arange(n) + 0.5)) * dx).sum()

for n in (10, 100, 1000):
    print(n, midpoint(np.sin, 0, np.pi, n))   # approaches 2

print(-np.cos(np.pi) + np.cos(0))             # exactly 2.0

Where It Can't Be Applied

  • f must be continuous: the formula applied blindly to 1/x² on [−1, 1] gives −1 − 1 = −2, which is impossible since the integrand is positive everywhere. The integrand blows up at 0, so the theorem does not apply.
  • F must exist in a usable form: e^(−x²) has an antiderivative, but none that can be written in terms of elementary functions. So the theorem does not help with computing it, and numerical methods or series are needed.

Rules of the Definite Integral

The theorem makes the following immediate:

  • Reversal: the integral from b to a is minus the integral from a to b.
  • Splitting: the integral from a to b plus the integral from b to c is the integral from a to c.
  • Linearity: ∫ (af + bg) = a∫f + b∫g.
  • Change of variables: with u = g(x), the integral of f(g(x)) g′(x) from a to b equals the integral of f(u) from g(a) to g(b).

Closing Thought

Differentiation and integration look like unrelated problems, one about slopes and the other about areas. The first fundamental theorem says that integration is really undoing differentiation. The second theorem completes the picture by looking at it from the other direction.
