Local Search: Hill Climbing and Simulated Annealing

Artificial Intelligence Optimization

May 09, 2020

In many problems the path to a solution is irrelevant. For the 8-queens problem, scheduling or circuit layout, only the final configuration matters. Local search algorithms keep a single current state and try to improve it, instead of exploring paths systematically.

Why Local Search

  • It uses very little memory, often constant.
  • It finds reasonable solutions in huge or continuous spaces where systematic search is hopeless.
  • It naturally solves optimization problems, where the goal is the best state according to an objective function.

The state space can be pictured as a landscape: location is the state, elevation is the objective value. Local search tries to find the highest peak.

Hill Climbing

Hill climbing moves to the neighbouring state with the best value, and stops when no neighbour is better. It is simple, fast and greedy.

It gets stuck in three ways:

  • Local maxima: a peak lower than the highest peak.
  • Ridges: sequences of local maxima that are hard to navigate with single moves.
  • Plateaus: flat regions where no neighbour is better.

On randomly generated 8-queens states, steepest-ascent hill climbing solves only about 14 percent of instances, though it is very fast when it does.

Escaping Local Optima

  • Sideways moves: allow moves to equal-valued neighbours to cross plateaus, with a limit.
  • Random restarts: run hill climbing from many random starting points and keep the best result. Given enough restarts, it finds a solution with probability approaching 1.
  • Stochastic hill climbing: choose randomly among uphill moves.

Simulated Annealing

Simulated annealing borrows from metallurgy, where metals are heated and slowly cooled to reach a low-energy crystal structure.

At each step it picks a random neighbour. If the move improves the state, it is accepted. If not, it is still accepted with probability:

P = e^(ΔE / T)

where ΔE is the change in value (negative for a worse move) and T is the temperature. Early on, T is high and bad moves are often accepted, letting the search escape local optima. T is lowered gradually, so the search becomes increasingly greedy.

If the temperature decreases slowly enough, simulated annealing finds a global optimum with probability approaching 1.

Local Beam Search

Instead of one state, local beam search keeps k states and selects the best k among all their successors, sharing information between parallel searches.

Closing Thought

Local search trades guarantees for scale. It cannot prove it found the best answer, but it can find a good one in spaces far too large to search exhaustively.
