The Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem

Mathematics Probability

Nov 16, 2024

Machine learning trusts averages. We report average losses, use mini-batch gradients and estimate integrals with samples. Two theorems explain why this is justified, and also what could go wrong.

The Law of Large Numbers

Take independent draws from a distribution with mean μ. The sample mean converges to the true mean:

x̄ₙ = (1/n) Σ xᵢ → μ as n → ∞

This is why training error can estimate an expected loss, and why more data gives better estimates. It is a statement about the limit only. It doesn't say how fast convergence happens.

Concentration

Bounds address the question of speed. Chebyshev's inequality needs only a finite variance:

P( |x̄ₙ − μ| ≥ ε ) ≤ σ² / (n ε²)

For bounded variables, Hoeffding's inequality gives an exponential rate:

P( |x̄ₙ − μ| ≥ ε ) ≤ 2 exp( −2nε² / (b − a)² )

This bound is the basis for many generalisation results, since it controls how far a measured accuracy may be from the true one.

The Central Limit Theorem

The law of large numbers says the mean settles. The central limit theorem describes the fluctuations around it. For independent, identically distributed draws with finite variance σ²:

√n (x̄ₙ − μ) / σ → N(0, 1)

Whatever the original distribution looks like, its sample means are approximately Gaussian, with standard deviation σ / √n.

The shape of the data is washed away. Only its mean and variance survive.

The Gaussian is the fixed point of this process: adding independent Gaussians gives another Gaussian, while every other finite-variance distribution is pulled toward it. 

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Exponential draws are heavily skewed, with mean 1 and variance 1
for n in (1, 5, 30, 500):
    means = rng.exponential(1.0, (20_000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    z = (means - 1.0) * np.sqrt(n)
    skew = (z**3).mean() / z.std()**3
    print(n, round(means.std(), 3), round(skew, 2))

The skewness falls roughly as 2 / √n, and the spread of the means falls as 1 / √n.

Rates and the Square Root

The factor √n has a practical consequence: halving the error takes four times the data. A hundred times more data gives one more digit of accuracy. This applies to Monte Carlo estimates, A/B tests and stochastic gradients alike.

It also gives a simple rule of thumb. An accuracy measured on 1,000 test examples, near 90%, has a standard error of √(0.9 · 0.1 / 1000), or about 0.95%. A 1% difference between two models on that test set may well be noise.

When It Breaks

  • Heavy tails: the Cauchy distribution has no mean, and the average of n Cauchy draws has the same distribution as a single draw. Averaging gains nothing.
  • Dependence: correlated samples, such as those from an MCMC chain, hold less information than their count suggests, so the effective sample size is smaller.
  • Non-stationary data: if the distribution drifts, an average converges to nothing in particular.
  • Small samples with rare events: with very skewed distributions, the Gaussian approximation may need far more data than the common advice of n = 30.

Why This Matters for Training

A mini-batch gradient is a sample mean of per-example gradients, so it is approximately Gaussian around the full gradient with a noise scale of σ / √B. Doubling batch size therefore reduces noise by only about 30%. This explains the appeal of small batches: many noisy steps tend to beat a few precise ones.

Closing Thought

The law of large numbers says averages can be trusted, and the central limit theorem says how much. Together they connect one measurement to its uncertainty, and they underlie almost every error bar in machine learning.
