The Bias-Variance Tradeoff

Machine Learning Model Selection

Jan 28, 2023

Why does a more powerful model sometimes predict worse? The bias-variance decomposition answers this by splitting test error into parts that have different causes and different cures.

The Decomposition

Assume y = f(x) + ε, where ε is noise with variance σ². Train a model f̂ on a random training set. Averaging over training sets, the expected squared error at a point x is:

E[(y − f̂(x))²] = (E[f̂(x)] − f(x))² + Var(f̂(x)) + σ²

  • Bias²: error from the model's average prediction being wrong, usually because it is too simple.
  • Variance: error from the model being sensitive to the particular training set.
  • Noise: error that no model can remove.

Bias is being consistently wrong. Variance is being inconsistently right.

Polynomials Make It Visible

Fit polynomials to noisy samples of a sine wave on many different training sets. A straight line barely changes between training sets but misses the curve everywhere. A degree 9 polynomial chases the training points and swings widely from one set to the next. 

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 25)
truth = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)

for degree in (1, 3, 9):
    preds = []
    for _ in range(300):
        y = truth + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 25)
        preds.append(np.polyval(np.polyfit(x, y, degree), x))
    preds = np.array(preds)
    bias2 = ((preds.mean(axis=0) - truth)**2).mean()
    variance = preds.var(axis=0).mean()
    print(degree, round(bias2, 3), round(variance, 3))

Bias falls and variance rises as the degree goes up. Test error, the sum of the two plus noise, typically traces a U shape with its minimum somewhere in the middle.

Reading the Symptoms

Comparing training and validation error tells you which side you are on:

  • Both errors high and close together: high bias. Use a more flexible model or better features. More data will not help much.
  • Low training error, much higher validation error: high variance. Get more data, regularise, reduce the model, or average several models.

Cross-Validation

Since we cannot measure bias and variance directly on real problems, we estimate test error instead. k-fold cross-validation splits the data into k folds, trains on k − 1 of them, validates on the remaining one, and averages across all k rotations. Values such as k = 5 or k = 10 are typical.

Two rules keep the estimate honest. All preprocessing must be fitted inside each fold, or validation data leaks into training. And after selecting hyperparameters using cross-validation, the winning score is optimistic, so a final held-out test set is needed for an unbiased estimate.

Levers on the Tradeoff

  • Regularisation: adds a little bias to remove more variance.
  • Bagging: averages models trained on resampled data, which mainly reduces variance.
  • Boosting: builds up a sequence of weak models, which mainly reduces bias.
  • More data: reduces variance without adding bias, which is why it is so often the best option.

When the U Curve Breaks

Very large neural networks can fit training data perfectly and still generalise well. Test error can even drop again as parameters grow past the point of exact interpolation, a pattern called double descent. The decomposition itself still holds as an identity. What changes is the idea that parameter count alone measures complexity, since training procedures such as stochastic gradient descent favour particular solutions among the many that fit.

Closing Thought

Treating error as one number hides what to do about it. Splitting it shows whether the fix is more capacity, more data or more constraint, and the most widely used form of constraint is the L2 penalty.
