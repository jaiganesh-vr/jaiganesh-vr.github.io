The Multivariate Gaussian

Mathematics Probability

Jun 22, 2024

The Gaussian appears throughout machine learning, in noise models, priors, latent spaces and mixture components. It is common partly because it arises naturally and largely because everything you might want to compute with it has an exact answer.

The Density

For a d-dimensional vector with mean μ and covariance Σ:

N(x; μ, Σ) = (2π)^(−d/2) · |Σ|^(−½) · exp( −½ (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ) )

The exponent is a quadratic form, and the term out front only normalises. The inverse covariance Σ⁻¹ is known as the precision.

The Geometry

Contours of equal density are ellipsoids, and eigendecomposition of the covariance describes their shape. With Σ = QΛQᵀ:

  • Directions of the axes: the eigenvectors in Q.
  • Length along each axis: proportional to √λᵢ.

The exponent is the squared Mahalanobis distance, which is a Euclidean distance after rotating to those axes and dividing each by √λᵢ. This leads to a useful view of any Gaussian.

Every Gaussian is a standard Gaussian that has been rotated, stretched and shifted.

So if z ~ N(0, I) and Σ = LLᵀ:

x = μ + L z gives x ~ N(μ, Σ)

This is the standard sampling method, and also the reparameterisation trick used in VAEs.

Closure Properties

Gaussians remain Gaussian under several important operations:

  • Linear maps: if x ~ N(μ, Σ), then Ax + b ~ N(Aμ + b, AΣAᵀ).
  • Sums: independent Gaussians add, with means and covariances adding.
  • Marginals: to marginalise, just drop the relevant rows and columns from μ and Σ.
  • Conditionals: conditioning on part of the vector gives a Gaussian, with a closed form.

Conditioning

Split x into two blocks, with the covariance split into Σ₁₁, Σ₁₂ and Σ₂₂. Then:

μ₁|₂ = μ₁ + Σ₁₂ Σ₂₂⁻¹ (x₂ − μ₂)

Σ₁|₂ = Σ₁₁ − Σ₁₂ Σ₂₂⁻¹ Σ₂₁

The mean shifts linearly with what was observed. The covariance shrinks by an amount that doesn't depend on the observed value. Gaussian processes and Kalman filters are mostly repeated applications of these two lines. 

import numpy as np

def condition(mu, S, x2):
    """Distribution of the first variable given the second, for a 2D Gaussian."""
    mean = mu[0] + S[0, 1] / S[1, 1] * (x2 - mu[1])
    var = S[0, 0] - S[0, 1]**2 / S[1, 1]
    return mean, var

mu = np.array([0.0, 0.0])
S = np.array([[1.0, 0.8], [0.8, 1.0]])
print(condition(mu, S, 1.0))           # (0.8, 0.36)

# Check by sampling, keeping draws where x2 is close to 1
x = np.random.default_rng(0).multivariate_normal(mu, S, 2_000_000)
near = x[np.abs(x[:, 1] - 1.0) < 0.01, 0]
print(near.mean().round(3), near.var().round(3))

Independence

For most distributions, zero correlation doesn't imply independence. For jointly Gaussian variables it does, because a diagonal Σ makes the density factor into a product. Similarly, a zero entry in the precision matrix means two variables are independent given all the others, and this gives Gaussian graphical models their structure.

Why It Is Everywhere

  • Central limit theorem: sums of many small independent effects tend toward it.
  • Maximum entropy: among all distributions with a given mean and covariance, it makes the fewest additional assumptions.
  • Least squares: Gaussian noise makes maximum likelihood equal to squared error.
  • Tractability: a Gaussian prior with a Gaussian likelihood gives a Gaussian posterior.

Where It Misleads

The tails fall off extremely quickly, so a single outlier can distort a fit, and heavier-tailed alternatives such as the Student t are more robust. In high dimensions, its intuition also breaks down, since almost all the probability mass is in a thin shell at radius about √d rather than near the peak.

Closing Thought

The Gaussian is popular because its algebra closes neatly on itself. Understanding it as a transformed standard Gaussian explains most of its formulas. Next, why sums of random variables end up looking like this.
