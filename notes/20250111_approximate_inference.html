Approximate Inference by Sampling

Artificial Intelligence Probability

Jan 11, 2025

Exact inference in Bayesian networks is NP-hard in general. For large networks, approximate inference estimates probabilities by generating samples, and the estimates improve as more samples are drawn.

Direct Sampling

The simplest method samples each variable in topological order, parents before children, drawing from its conditional distribution given the parents' sampled values. Each run produces one possible world, drawn with exactly the probability the network assigns to it.

To estimate P(Rain = true), count the fraction of samples where it is true. By the law of large numbers, the estimate converges to the true probability.

Rejection Sampling

To estimate a conditional probability, such as P(Rain | WetGrass), generate samples and discard those inconsistent with the evidence.

It is simple and correct, but wasteful. If the evidence is rare, almost every sample is rejected, and the number of usable samples drops exponentially with the number of evidence variables.

Likelihood Weighting

Likelihood weighting fixes the evidence variables to their observed values and samples only the others. Each sample is weighted by the likelihood of the evidence given its sampled parents.

  • Every sample is used, none rejected.
  • Weighted counts give consistent estimates.
  • Performance degrades when evidence appears late in the ordering, because the sampled values ignore it.

Rejection sampling throws away mismatches. Likelihood weighting never creates them.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo

MCMC takes a different approach. It starts from an arbitrary state consistent with the evidence and repeatedly makes small random changes, forming a Markov chain whose long-run distribution is the posterior.

Gibbs sampling is the most common form:

  • Pick a non-evidence variable.
  • Resample its value from its distribution given its Markov blanket: its parents, children and children's other parents.
  • Repeat many times, recording the states visited.

The fraction of time spent in each state approximates its posterior probability. Early samples, before the chain settles, are discarded as burn-in.

Choosing a Method

  • Likelihood weighting: easy to implement, good when evidence is near the top of the network.
  • Gibbs sampling: effective for large networks and evidence anywhere, but can mix slowly when variables are strongly correlated.

These ideas reach far beyond Bayesian networks. MCMC is a workhorse of Bayesian statistics, physics and modern probabilistic programming.

Closing Thought

Sampling turns intractable sums into repeated simulation. It trades exactness for scalability, and gives answers whose precision can be improved simply by running longer.
