Factorization Into A = LU

Mathematics Linear Algebra

Sep 18, 2021

Elimination produces an upper triangular matrix U, and the multipliers it uses along the way are usually discarded. Keeping them turns the method into a factorisation of A into two triangular matrices.

From Elimination to LU

Elimination applies a sequence of matrices to A:

E₃₂ E₃₁ E₂₁ A = U

Moving them to the other side means inverting them and reversing their order:

A = E₂₁⁻¹ E₃₁⁻¹ E₃₂⁻¹ U = L U

Each inverse is easy, since undoing "subtract 3 times row 1" means adding 3 times row 1, which only flips the sign of one entry.

Why L Is So Clean

The product in the forward direction, E, is messy, while the product of the inverses, L, is not. Take the elimination steps on our example, where the multipliers were 3 and 2:

E = E₃₂E₂₁ = [[1, 0, 0], [−3, 1, 0], [6, −2, 1]]

L = E⁻¹ = [[1, 0, 0], [3, 1, 0], [0, 2, 1]]

E has an entry of 6, because row 1 affects row 2, which then affects row 3. In L, the multipliers simply sit in their own positions, with no interaction between them.

L is the record of elimination: each multiplier is written down where it was used.

For our matrix, this gives:

[[1, 2, 1], [3, 8, 1], [0, 4, 1]] = [[1, 0, 0], [3, 1, 0], [0, 2, 1]] · [[1, 2, 1], [0, 2, −2], [0, 0, 5]]

Computing It

Since L has ones on its diagonal, only the entries below the diagonal need storing. They fit in the zeros that elimination creates, so the factorisation can overwrite A in place: 

import numpy as np

def lu(A):
    """Doolittle LU without pivoting. Returns unit lower L and upper U."""
    A = A.astype(float)
    n = len(A)
    for k in range(n - 1):
        A[k + 1:, k] /= A[k, k]                                  # multipliers
        A[k + 1:, k + 1:] -= np.outer(A[k + 1:, k], A[k, k + 1:])
    return np.tril(A, -1) + np.eye(n), np.triu(A)

A = np.array([[1, 2, 1], [3, 8, 1], [0, 4, 1]])
L, U = lu(A)
print(L)
print(U)
print(np.allclose(L @ U, A))   # True

Why It Is Worth Having

The factorisation separates the work on A from the work on b. Solving Ax = b splits into two triangular solves:

  • Forward substitution: solve Lc = b for c.
  • Back substitution: solve Ux = c for x.

The factorisation costs about n³/3 operations, but each new right-hand side costs only about n². With a 1,000 × 1,000 matrix and 100 right-hand sides, redoing elimination every time would take about 33 billion operations for the elimination alone. Factorising once takes about 330 million, plus about 100 million for all the triangular solves.

The same reasoning is why computing A⁻¹ is rarely needed. A stored LU does everything an inverse would, at lower cost and with better accuracy.

Row Exchanges

If a zero or very small pivot turns up, rows must be exchanged. All the exchanges can be collected in a permutation matrix P, which reorders the rows of A in advance:

P A = L U

Every invertible matrix has this form, and it is what standard library routines compute. Without exchanges, some invertible matrices have no LU at all. [[0, 1], [1, 0]] is one, since its first pivot is zero.

The LDU Form

U has the pivots on its diagonal, whereas L has ones. Dividing the pivots out of U restores the symmetry between the two factors:

A = L D U, where U now has ones on its diagonal

For a symmetric matrix, the factorisation simplifies to A = L D Lᵀ, which needs only half of the storage and work.

Structure Is Preserved

A band matrix, whose nonzero entries are close to the diagonal, keeps its bandwidth in L and U. For a tridiagonal system, the cost falls from n³/3 to about n. This is why large sparse systems, such as those from physical simulations, can be solved at all. The inverse of such a matrix is generally full, which is another reason to keep the factors.

Closing Thought

LU is elimination with a memory. It is a small change in viewpoint with a large practical payoff, and it also makes visible the row operations that have been taking place all along.
