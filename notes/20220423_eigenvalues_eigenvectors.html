Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Mathematics Linear Algebra

Apr 23, 2022

Under a matrix transformation, most vectors change direction. A special few only get stretched or shrunk. These vectors expose the natural axes of the transformation, and using them turns a complicated matrix into something simple.

The Definition

An eigenvector v of a square matrix A keeps its direction when transformed, and the eigenvalue λ is the factor by which it scales:

A v = λ v

Finding Them

Rearranging gives (A − λI) v = 0. A nonzero solution exists only if A − λI is singular:

det(A − λI) = 0

Take A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. Expanding gives (2 − λ)² − 1 = 0, so λ = 3 or λ = 1. The eigenvectors are (1, 1) and (1, −1) respectively. The matrix stretches by 3 along one diagonal and leaves the other unchanged in length. For large matrices, nobody uses this polynomial in practice, since numerical algorithms are far more stable.

Two useful checks: the sum of the eigenvalues is the trace, and their product is the determinant. Here 3 + 1 = 4 and 3 · 1 = 3, as expected.

Diagonalisation

If A has n independent eigenvectors, collect them as the columns of V, with eigenvalues on the diagonal of Λ:

A = V Λ V⁻¹

This reads as: change to the eigenvector coordinates, scale each axis, and change back. Powers then become easy, as the inner factors cancel:

Aᵏ = V Λᵏ V⁻¹

In the right coordinates, a matrix is only scaling.

Symmetric Matrices

Covariance matrices, Hessians and Gram matrices are all symmetric, and the spectral theorem makes these particularly well-behaved. A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors:

A = Q Λ Qᵀ

Thus the transformation is a rotation, a scaling along perpendicular axes, and a rotation back. PCA relies on exactly this structure, applied to the covariance matrix.

Power Iteration

Applying A repeatedly amplifies each eigenvector component by its eigenvalue, so the component with the largest eigenvalue comes to dominate. Multiplying and normalising is enough to find it: 

import numpy as np

def power_iteration(A, steps=100, seed=0):
    v = np.random.default_rng(seed).normal(size=A.shape[0])
    for _ in range(steps):
        v = A @ v
        v /= np.linalg.norm(v)
    return v @ A @ v, v      # Rayleigh quotient, eigenvector

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0]])
value, vector = power_iteration(A)
print(round(value, 6), vector.round(4))  # 3.0, along (1, 1)
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(A))             # [1. 3.]

Convergence is governed by the ratio of the two largest eigenvalues. PageRank is essentially this procedure at web scale.

What Eigenvalues Tell Us

  • Stability: repeatedly applying a matrix grows vectors if its largest absolute eigenvalue exceeds 1 and shrinks them if it is below 1. This is the reason recurrent networks can have exploding and vanishing gradients.
  • Curvature: the eigenvalues of a Hessian give curvature along each axis, which determines how hard an optimisation problem is.
  • Conditioning: the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue measures how sensitive a symmetric system is to small errors.
  • Markov chains: a stationary distribution is a left eigenvector with eigenvalue 1.

Limits and the SVD

Not every matrix has a full set of eigenvectors, and most rotations in two dimensions have no real eigenvectors at all. Non-square matrices are not covered in the first place. The singular value decomposition resolves this by giving every matrix a similar factorisation, and its singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of AᵀA.

Closing Thought

Eigenvectors cut through the complexity of a transformation by finding the directions in which it acts simply. When the eigenvalues are all positive, the matrix has a particularly useful property.
