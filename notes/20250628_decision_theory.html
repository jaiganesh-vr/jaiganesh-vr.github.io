Decision Theory and Utility

Artificial Intelligence Decisions

Jun 28, 2025

Probability tells an agent what to believe. It does not say what to do. Decision theory combines beliefs with preferences, giving a principled way to choose actions under uncertainty.

Utility

A utility function assigns a number to each state, expressing how desirable it is to the agent. Only the ordering and relative differences matter: multiplying all utilities by a positive constant, or adding a constant, does not change any decision.

Maximum Expected Utility

The expected utility of an action is the average utility of its outcomes, weighted by their probabilities:

EU(a) = Σ P(s' | a) · U(s')

A rational agent chooses the action with maximum expected utility. This single principle defines rational behavior under uncertainty.

Decision theory = probability theory + utility theory.

Why Utilities Exist

The axioms of utility theory state basic requirements for rational preferences, such as transitivity: if you prefer A to B and B to C, you must prefer A to C. Otherwise you could be led around a cycle, paying a little at each step. It can be proved that any agent whose preferences satisfy these axioms behaves as if maximising some utility function.

Money Is Not Utility

People do not value money linearly. Most would take a certain 1 million over a coin flip for 2.5 million, even though the gamble's expected monetary value is higher. The utility of money is roughly logarithmic: each extra dollar matters less the more you have.

  • Risk-averse: prefers a sure thing to a gamble with the same expected value.
  • Risk-neutral: indifferent between them.
  • Risk-seeking: prefers the gamble.

Multi-Attribute Utility

Real decisions involve many attributes: cost, safety, time, comfort. Multi-attribute utility theory combines them, often as a weighted sum when attributes are independent in the agent's preferences. Sometimes one option dominates another on every attribute, and the decision is easy.

Value of Information

Gathering information has costs, so an agent must decide when it is worth it. The value of perfect information is the difference between the expected utility of deciding after learning something and the expected utility of deciding now.

  • Information is valuable only if it might change the decision.
  • Its value is never negative for a rational agent, in expectation.

A doctor should order a test only if its result could change the treatment, and its expected benefit exceeds its cost.

Decision Networks

Decision networks extend Bayesian networks with decision nodes and utility nodes, so that complex decisions can be represented and solved with the same graphical tools.

Closing Thought

Maximum expected utility is the definition of a rational agent made precise. The challenge in practice is specifying utilities that truly reflect what we want, a problem that remains central in AI today.
